N5 Grammar

です / だ

Polite

Shirokuma Cafe · Season 1, Episode 1

ゾウガメです

"[I'm] a Galapagos tortoise."

です says what something is, the polite way. It goes right after a noun (and after な-adjectives, covered later): thing + です = "it's (a) thing".

He's introducing himself, so ゾウガメです answers "What are you?": "I'm a Galapagos tortoise." There's no word for "I". It's clear who he means, so Japanese leaves it out. That's what the [brackets] show.

Use it with strangers, clients and shops. With friends you'd say the casual version, ゾウガメだ: same meaning.

Casual

New Atashin'chi · Episode 1

ただの落書きだ

"[It's] just a scribble."

Casual version: だ instead of です. Same job: it answers "What's that?" (ただの is "just a", 落書き is "scribble"). Use it with friends, family, or when thinking out loud.

Examples

A client asks what the sketch on your screen is.

これは表紙です。

"This is the cover."

Your friend asks what the sketch on your screen is, over coffee.

表紙だよ。

"It's the cover."

Someone at the post office asks where you're from.

オランダ人です。

"I'm Dutch."

You open the curtains in the morning and say it to yourself.

雨だ。

"It's raining."

With and without

です

雨です。

Polite. Strangers, clients, shops.

だ

雨だ。

Casual. Friends, or thinking out loud when you look outside.

Dropped

雨！

Super casual. Just the word. Totally normal with friends.

With よ

雨だよ。

"It's raining, FYI." Telling someone something they don't know.

な

Source unknown

私の友達は有名な会計士です

"My friend is a famous accountant."

な connects a な-adjective to the noun it describes. It goes right between them: 有名 + な + 会計士 = "famous accountant".

The adjective answers "What kind of accountant?": a famous one.

Only な-adjectives need it. い-adjectives connect directly: 高い車, "an expensive car". At the end of a sentence there's no な: 会計士は有名です.

Examples

"It's a quiet room."

Someone asks about your favorite food.

好きな食べ物はカレーです。

"My favorite food is curry."

A friend asks why you never order ramen.

ラーメンが嫌いなんです。

"I don't like ramen, that's why."

With and without

い-word

重い箱

"A heavy box." Connects directly, no glue.

With な

静かな部屋

"A quiet room." Needs な to connect.

N5 Grammar

は

Shirokuma Cafe · Season 1, Episode 5

それは剣道

"That [is] kendo."

は marks the topic: what the sentence is about. It goes right after a noun, and then you say something about it: それ + は + 剣道 = "as for that: kendo".

It answers "What's that?". それは sets the topic ("that"), 剣道 is what gets said about it. There's no だ at the end: in casual speech it often gets dropped, which is what the [brackets] show.

Written は, said "wa". Once everyone knows the topic, it gets dropped too: just 剣道 would also work as an answer.

Examples

Meeting a new client. You introduce yourself.

私はセブです。

"As for me, I'm Seb."

A friend asks if you're free this week.

今日は忙しい。明日は大丈夫。

"Today, I'm busy. Tomorrow, I'm fine."

At dinner, someone offers you meat and fish.

魚は食べます。

"Fish, I do eat." (meat, maybe not)

With and without

Without

魚、食べる。

"I eat fish." Plain and casual.

With は

魚は食べる。

"Fish, I eat." Hints there's something else you don't.

N5 Grammar

が

Source unknown

桜が満開です

"The cherry blossoms are in full bloom."

が marks who or what does something or is in some state, when that's new information. It goes right after the noun: 桜 + が + 満開です.

You notice something new: the blossoms are out. The sentence answers "What's happening?", and が presents 桜 as the news.

With は (桜は満開です) it would mean "as for the cherry blossoms, they're in full bloom": talking about blossoms you were already discussing, not announcing them.

Examples

Someone at an exhibition asks who made this illustration.

私が描きました。

"I drew it." (me, the one standing here)

You look out the window and notice something.

あ、雨が降ってる！

"Oh, it's raining!"

A friend asks what kind of animals you like.

猫が好き。

"I like cats."

は or が?

With は

私はセブです。

Introducing yourself. "Me? I'm Seb."

With が

私がセブです。

A client looks around asking "which one is Seb?" You raise your hand: "I'm Seb."

N5 Grammar

を

Source unknown

炭酸水を買います

"[I'll] buy sparkling water."

を marks the object: the thing the action is done to. It goes right after that noun, before the verb: 炭酸水 + を + 買います.

買います is "buy", and を tells you what gets bought. It answers "Buy what?": sparkling water. There's no "I": it's clear who's buying, so Japanese leaves it out (the [brackets]).

Written を, said "o". In casual speech it's often dropped: 炭酸水買う.

Examples

A friend texts asking what you're up to.

表紙を描いてる。

"Drawing a cover."

At the konbini counter.

コーヒーをください。

"A coffee, please."

Walking the beach in Chigasaki with your camera.

海の写真を撮る。

"I'm taking photos of the sea."

With and without

With を

コーヒーを飲みますか？

Polite, complete. Shops, clients.

Dropped

コーヒー飲む？

With friends を often just disappears. Totally fine.

N5 Grammar

も

YouTube · video

クロワッサンも好きです

"I like croissants too."

も means "too, also". It goes right after a noun, in the spot where は, が or を would be: クロワッサン + も + 好きです.

It answers "anything else?". She already said what she likes, and croissants get added to that list.

も takes the place of は, が and を, so never はも or がも. With に or で it goes after them: 箱根にも, "in Hakone too".

Examples

After a long hike, your friend says they're exhausted.

私も疲れた。

"I'm tired too."

At a bakery, you've picked a melon bun and point at one more.

これもください。

"This one too, please."

It rained all day. You check the forecast.

明日も雨だ。

"Tomorrow's rain too."

With and without

With は

私は行きます。

"I'm going." Just about you.

With も

私も行きます。

"I'm going too." Someone else is already going.

N5 Grammar

か

Polite

YouTube · video

レジ袋いりますか？

"Do you need a bag?" [the cashier, at the till]

か at the end turns a sentence into a question. It's a spoken question mark.

It goes right after the polite ending: いります → いりますか, です → ですか. Here the cashier asks a yes/no question: bag or no bag?

In casual speech you usually drop か and just raise your voice: いる？

Casual

"What is this? Some kind of trap?"

Casual か goes straight after a verb or noun, with no だ: 罠か, never 罠だか. It sounds blunt, so you mostly hear it from guys, or from someone asking themself.

Examples

You're on a platform, not sure about the train. You ask the person next to you.

この電車は渋谷に行きますか？

"Does this train go to Shibuya?"

Polite

学生ですか？

"Are you a student?"

Polite

Your phone died and you ask a shop clerk.

今何時ですか？

"What time is it?"

Polite, with a question word

Texting a friend about tomorrow's barbecue.

明日来る？

"You coming tomorrow?"

Casual, no か

Without

N5 Grammar

これ・それ・あれ

YouTube · video

これは急須です

"This is a teapot."

これ, それ and あれ mean "this thing" or "that thing". Which one you pick depends on where the thing is: これ is near me, それ is near you, あれ is far from both of us.

She's holding the teapot in her hand, so it's これ. If you were holding it, she'd say それは急須です.

They work like normal nouns, so particles go right after: これは, それが, あれを.

Examples

At the konbini, you hold up an onigiri you don't recognise.

これは何ですか？

"What's this?"

It's in your hand: これ.

A friend shows you their new camera.

それ、いくらだった？

"How much was that?"

It's in their hand: それ.

On the beach in Chigasaki, you spot an island far out at sea.

あれは江の島？

"Is that Enoshima?"

Far from both of you: あれ.

A friend says they're moving to Kyoto.

それはいいね。

"That's great."

それ also points at something the other person just said.

With and without

With これ

これは何ですか？

Points at the thing. Clear even if there are several things around.

Without

何ですか？

When you're already holding it up, Japanese often just leaves これ out.

N5 Grammar

～んです・のです

Polite

He Who Can't Marry · Episode 1

だから１階建てなんです！

"That's why it's a single-story building!"

んです adds "here's the reason or the story behind it". It goes after a verb or adjective in plain form. After a noun or な-adjective, add な: １階建て + な + んです.

He's explained the background, and this is the conclusion. だから is "that's why", and なんです marks the whole thing as an explanation. It answers "Why is it like that?". Without it, １階建てです is just a plain fact.

This is polite. Casual: １階建てなんだ. In writing: １階建てなのです.

Casual

Shirokuma Cafe · Season 1, Episode 2

これ試しに作ってみたんだ。

"[You see,] I tried making this."

Casual version: んだ instead of んです. Same job: he shows what he made and gives the story behind it. Use it with friends and family.

Examples

You show up late. Your friend gives you a look.

電車が遅れたんです。

"The train was late, that's why."

Explaining

You're carrying a box of art supplies, way behind your friend. They turn around like "come on!"

重いんです！

"It's heavy, okay!"

Explaining

A client keeps pointing at the red cover option.

赤がいいんですか？

"Oh, so you want red?" (asking about something you noticed)

Asking

A friend offers you the last piece of cake.

いいんですか？

"Are you sure? Is that really okay?"

With いいですか？ you're asking for permission. With いいんですか？ you're reacting to something.

Asking

You show a friend your illustration. They laugh: "no way, you didn't draw that, you used AI."

本当に描いたんです！

"I really did draw it!" You're pushing back on what they think.

Pushing back

You come back from the beach and tell a friend what you saw.

富士山がすごくきれいだったんです！

"Mt. Fuji was SO beautiful!" Sharing a feeling and pulling them into it.

Sharing a feeling

With and without

Without

重いです。

Just a fact. Someone asks how heavy the box is, you report it.

With んです

重いんです。

The reason for what's going on. That's why I'm slow.

Without

赤がいいですか？

Neutral offer, out of nowhere. A waiter asking "red or white?"

With んです

赤がいいんですか？

You noticed something and you're checking. Can sound a bit surprised.

Without

描きました。

"I drew it." Just reporting.

With んです

描いたんです！

"I DID draw it!" Insisting, because you're reacting to something in the air (their doubt).

Casual: んだ and の

Same thing, just casual. In games, anime and with friends you'll hear these far more than んです.

You show up late to meet a friend.

電車が遅れたんだ。

"The train was late, that's why."

Your friend looks upset.

どうしたの？

"What happened?"

Polite

遅れたんです。

To a client or a stranger.

Casual

遅れたんだ。

To a friend.

Polite

どうしたんですか？

To a client or a stranger.

Casual

どうしたの？

To a friend.

Written: のです

Same thing in its full form. You'll see it in books and articles. In conversation it can sound a bit stiff.

Spoken

遅れたんです。

What you'll hear almost always.

Written

遅れたのです。

Books, articles, formal writing.

When to add な

With nouns and な-adjectives, add な. Verbs and い-adjectives get nothing. More about な

A friend asks why you never order ramen.

ラーメンが嫌いなんです。

"I don't like ramen, that's why."

Verb / い-adjective

遅れたんです

Nothing added.

Noun / な-adjective

嫌いなんです

Add な.

Casual

このジャグ　どうしたの？

"Where did this dispenser come from?"

の at the end of a question asks for the story behind something you noticed. It goes after a verb or adjective in plain form (after nouns and な-adjectives, add な: 学生なの？).

They spot a drink dispenser that wasn't there before. Literally "this jug, what happened?". It asks "What's the story here?".

This is casual. Polite: どうしたんですか？ Without の, どうした？ sounds blunter, just "what happened?".

The full form is の + ですか = のですか. Said quickly, the の gets squashed into ん, so you get んですか. Same meaning, both correct. In conversation it's almost always んですか. のですか is for books and articles, and said out loud it sounds stiff, like a news reader.

N5 Grammar

のは・のが

Source unknown

昼間に山道を歩くのは楽しいです

"Walking along the mountain path during the day is fun."

の turns an action into a thing, like "-ing" in English. It goes right after a verb in plain form: 歩く (walk) + の = "walking".

昼間に山道を歩く is "walk the mountain path in the daytime". Add の and the whole action becomes one thing, は makes it the topic, and 楽しいです says what it's like. It answers "How is it, walking there?".

のが works the same way with words like 好き and 得意: 歩くのが好き, "I like walking".

Examples

A friend asks what you do on weekends. You love swimming.

泳ぐのが好き。

"I like swimming."

の works like English "-ing": swim becomes swimming.

の = the act

Your alarm goes off at 5am, it's dark and cold.

朝早く起きるのはつらい。

"Getting up early is rough."

The whole chunk 朝早く起きる becomes one thing, then は: as for that, it's rough.

の = the act

At a party, someone asks who made the cake.

ケーキを作ったのは母だ。

"The one who made the cake is my mom."

Everyone knows someone made it. The answer comes last, like a reveal.

の = the one

Back from a trip to Japan, a friend asks about the food.

一番おいしかったのはラーメンだった。

"The thing that was tastiest was the ramen."

の = the thing

Note · 27 Jan 2025

ひらがな・カタカナ

The Japanese written language is a fusion of three distinct scripts. Children in Japan first learn Hiragana and Katakana, before progressing to the more complex and vast set of Kanji characters, which were imported from China around the 5th century CE.

Phonetics

Hiragana and Katakana are phonetic scripts, meaning they visually represent sounds used in speech and don't carry any meaning. While both character sets largely share the same basic syllables, Katakana includes additional characters for representing foreign sounds not traditionally found in Japanese. Both scripts serve different purposes within the language. Although not used this way in practice, you could technically spell native Japanese words using just these two scripts.

Memorizing characters using mnemonics

I found memorizing Hiragana and Katakana fairly easy, and it took me about a week. What worked best was utilizing visual mnemonics that simplify remembering information through simple associations.

Here's an example for memorizing the character 'の'. This character represents a syllable roughly pronounced as 'no' in the English word 'not'. Associating it with a 'No/not smoking' sign, which is similar in shape to the hiragana character, was a helpful visual cue.

The internet is full of examples of mnemonics for the kana, so there is no need to make them up yourself.

Learning how to write

Learning to write Hiragana and Katakana isn't too challenging, but it's important to follow the correct stroke order. Correct stroke order is crucial because it helps you intuitively write new characters and improves readability.

I started with some practice sheets to learn the stroke order, and then switched to a gridded notebook, writing the characters from memory.

Practice, practice, practice

During my first month in Japan back in 2020, I regularly did speed quizzes to recognize kana as quickly as possible. Pretty soon, the characters came naturally, and eventually, I didn't have to rely on mnemonics. Reading Hiragana and Katakana now feels as familiar as reading the Latin script; I simply know their shapes and sounds without having to think about them.

Practical use in daily life

Hiragana is mostly used for Japanese-origin words, so without knowing the meanings of those words, it wasn't very useful to me until I started learning some actual vocabulary.

The most useful thing for me right from the start was knowing Katakana. This script is primarily used for foreign loanwords, often imported from English, making them easy to understand. For instance, when I was searching for shampoo at a convenience store, I picked up a bottle with the text 'シャンプー' on it. These syllables spell 'sha-n-puu', making it likely I had found the right product. Although only a small step, it started making things easier for me and marked my beginning of learning Japanese vocabulary.
