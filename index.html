N5 Grammar

です / だ

Polite

Shirokuma Cafe · Season 1, Episode 5

あ、いいえ試作品です

"Oh, no, [it's] a prototype."

です says what something is, the polite way. It goes right after a noun (and after な-adjectives, covered later): thing + です = "it's (a) thing".

Someone guessed wrong, so 試作品です answers "What is it, then?": "It's a prototype." There's no word for "it". It's clear what he means, so Japanese leaves it out. That's what the [brackets] show.

Use it with strangers, clients and shops. With friends you'd say the casual version, 試作品だ: same meaning.

Casual

New Atashin'chi · Episode 1

ただの落書きだ

"[It's] just a scribble."

Casual version: だ instead of です. Same job: it answers "What's that?" (ただの is "just a", 落書き is "scribble"). Use it with friends, family, or when thinking out loud.

Examples

A client asks what the sketch on your screen is.

これは表紙です。

"This is the cover."

Your friend asks what the sketch on your screen is, over coffee.

表紙だよ。

"It's the cover."

Someone at the post office asks where you're from.

オランダ人です。

"I'm Dutch."

You open the curtains in the morning and say it to yourself.

雨だ。

"It's raining."

With and without

です

雨です。

Polite. Strangers, clients, shops.

だ

雨だ。

Casual. Friends, or thinking out loud when you look outside.

Dropped

雨！

Super casual. Just the word. Totally normal with friends.

With よ

雨だよ。

"It's raining, FYI." Telling someone something they don't know.

N5 Grammar

じゃない・ではありません

Polite

Pokémon: Indigo League · Episode 1

ボウヤじゃありません。

"[I'm] not a little boy."

じゃない says what something is NOT. It's the opposite of です / だ, and it goes in the same spot: right after a noun. Thing + じゃない = "it's not (a) thing".

He's been called ボウヤ ("little boy") and pushes back: ボウヤじゃありません, "I'm not a little boy." He's talking to an adult, so he says it politely.

Casual: じゃない. Polite: じゃないです or じゃありません. In writing and formal speech you'll see ではありません. All the same meaning.

Casual

Yu-Gi-Oh! · Season 0, Episode 1

はいおしまい、これは売り物じゃないんでな

"Okay, that's it. This isn't for sale."

Casual version: じゃない. Grandpa shuts down a customer: 売り物 ("something for sale") + じゃない = "not for sale". The んでな after it just means "so, you see".

Examples

A client points at a sketch and asks if it's the final cover.

いいえ、表紙じゃありません。

"No, it's not the cover."

Your friend asks the same thing over coffee.

表紙じゃないよ。

"It's not the cover."

Someone at the post office guesses you're German.

ドイツ人じゃないです。オランダ人です。

"I'm not German. I'm Dutch."

You look outside. The ground is wet, but nothing's falling.

雨じゃない。

"It's not raining."

With and without

だ

雨だ。

"It's raining." Says what it is.

じゃない

雨じゃない。

"It's not raining." Says what it isn't. Casual.

です

雨です。

"It's raining." Polite.

じゃないです / じゃありません

雨じゃありません。

"It's not raining." Polite. じゃないです is a bit softer and more everyday.

N5 Grammar

でした・だった

Polite

YouTube · Let's learn Japanese in the city!!

これは昔、山形県の県庁でした。

"This used to be the Yamagata prefectural office."

でした is the past of です: it says what something was. It goes in the same spot, right after a noun. Thing + でした = "it was (a) thing".

The guide stands in front of an old building and tells you what it used to be. 昔 ("long ago") sets the time, でした closes it in the past.

Polite: でした. Casual: だった, the past of だ. Same meaning.

Casual

New Atashin'chi · Episode 1

"See? Piece of cake... [it] was."

Casual version: だった. Mom squeezes onto a packed train: 楽勝 ("a breeze") + だった = "it was a breeze". Her squashed face says otherwise.

"It rained the whole time."

Literally "it was rain the whole time".

"Oh, it was free."

N5 Grammar

は

Shirokuma Cafe · Season 1, Episode 5

それは剣道

"That [is] kendo."

は marks the topic: what the sentence is about. It goes right after a noun, and then you say something about it: それ + は + 剣道 = "as for that: kendo".

It answers "What's that?". それは sets the topic ("that"), 剣道 is what gets said about it. There's no だ at the end: in casual speech it often gets dropped, which is what the [brackets] show.

Written は, said "wa". Once everyone knows the topic, it gets dropped too: just 剣道 would also work as an answer.

Examples

Meeting a new client. You introduce yourself.

私はセブです。

"As for me, I'm Seb."

A friend asks if you're free this week.

今日は忙しい。明日は大丈夫。

"Today, I'm busy. Tomorrow, I'm fine."

At dinner, someone offers you meat and fish.

魚は食べます。

"Fish, I do eat." (meat, maybe not)

With and without

Without

魚、食べる。

"I eat fish." Plain and casual.

With は

魚は食べる。

"Fish, I eat." Hints there's something else you don't.

N5 Grammar

か

Polite

YouTube · video

レジ袋いりますか？

"Do you need a bag?" [the cashier, at the till]

か at the end turns a sentence into a question. It's a spoken question mark.

It goes right after the polite ending: いります → いりますか, です → ですか. Here the cashier asks a yes/no question: bag or no bag?

In casual speech you usually drop か and just raise your voice: いる？

Casual

New Atashin'chi · Episode 1

しとめたか？

"Did I get it?"

Casual か goes straight after a plain verb or a noun, with no です and no だ: しとめたか, 罠か (never 罠だか). It sounds blunt, so you mostly hear it from guys, or from someone asking themself.

Examples

You're on a platform, not sure about the train. You ask the person next to you.

この電車は渋谷に行きますか？

"Does this train go to Shibuya?"

Polite

At a party, someone you just met mentions their exams.

学生ですか？

"Are you a student?"

Polite

Your phone died and you ask a shop clerk.

今何時ですか？

"What time is it?"

Polite, with a question word

Texting a friend about tomorrow's barbecue.

明日来る？

"You coming tomorrow?"

Casual, no か

With and without

Without

レジ袋いります。

"I need a bag." You're telling the cashier.

With か

レジ袋いりますか？

"Do you need a bag?" The cashier is asking you.

Casual, no か

袋いる？

"Need a bag?" Normal, friendly. Just a rising voice.

Casual with か

袋いるか？

"Need a bag or not?" Blunt, like a gruff dad.

N5 Grammar

これ・それ・あれ

YouTube · video

これは急須です

"This is a teapot."

これ, それ and あれ mean "this thing" or "that thing". Which one you pick depends on where the thing is: これ is near me, それ is near you, あれ is far from both of us.

She's holding the teapot in her hand, so it's これ. If you were holding it, she'd say それは急須です.

They work like normal nouns, so particles go right after: これは, それが, あれを.

Examples

At the konbini, you hold up an onigiri you don't recognise.

これは何ですか？

"What's this?"

It's in your hand: これ.

A friend shows you their new camera.

それ、いくらだった？

"How much was that?"

It's in their hand: それ.

On the beach in Chigasaki, you spot an island far out at sea.

あれは江の島？

"Is that Enoshima?"

Far from both of you: あれ.

A friend says they're moving to Kyoto.

それはいいね。

"That's great."

それ also points at something the other person just said.

With and without

With これ

これは何ですか？

Points at the thing. Clear even if there are several things around.

Without

何ですか？

When you're already holding it up, Japanese often just leaves これ out.

N5 Grammar

この・その・あの

YouTube · video

この自転車は借りた自転車です。

"This bike is a rented bike."

この, その and あの mean "this ___" or "that ___", and a noun always comes right after: この自転車, その本, あの人. Which one depends on where the thing is: この is near me, その is near you, あの is far from both of us.

He's sitting on the bike, so it's この自転車. It answers "which bike?": this one, the one I'm on.

They never stand alone. Without a noun you use これ, それ, あれ instead: これは借りた自転車です.

Examples

At a bakery, you point at a bread in the case right in front of you.

このパンをください。

"This bread, please."

Near you: この.

A friend is wearing a jacket you like.

そのジャケット、どこで買ったの？

"Where did you buy that jacket?"

It's on them: その.

You and a friend see a tall tower in the distance.

あのビルは何？

"What's that building?"

Far from both of you: あの.

A friend mentions a ramen place they went to last week.

その店、どこ？

"Where's that place?"

その also points at something the other person just talked about.

With and without

Without a noun

これは高いです。

"This is expensive." これ is the thing itself.

With この

この時計は高いです。

"This watch is expensive." この needs a noun after it.

Without a noun

あれは何？

"What's that?" You don't say what kind of thing.

With あの

あの鳥は何？

"What's that bird?" You say which thing: the bird.

YouTube · video

Polite

"This is a work that blends painting and manga."

Polite

"Um, before I go on a trip abroad, I always come to this place."

N5 Grammar

のは・のが

Source unknown

昼間に山道を歩くのは楽しいです

"Walking along the mountain path during the day is fun."

の turns an action into a thing, like "-ing" in English. It goes right after a verb in plain form: 歩く (walk) + の = "walking".

昼間に山道を歩く is "walk the mountain path in the daytime". Add の and the whole action becomes one thing, は makes it the topic, and 楽しいです says what it's like. It answers "How is it, walking there?".

のが works the same way with words like 好き and 得意: 歩くのが好き, "I like walking".

Examples

A friend asks what you do on weekends. You love swimming.

泳ぐのが好き。

"I like swimming."

の works like English "-ing": swim becomes swimming.

の = the act

Your alarm goes off at 5am, it's dark and cold.

朝早く起きるのはつらい。

"Getting up early is rough."

The whole chunk 朝早く起きる becomes one thing, then は: as for that, it's rough.

の = the act

At a party, someone asks who made the cake.

ケーキを作ったのは母だ。

"The one who made the cake is my mom."

Everyone knows someone made it. The answer comes last, like a reveal.

の = the one

Back from a trip to Japan, a friend asks about the food.

一番おいしかったのはラーメンだった。

"The thing that was tastiest was the ramen."

の = the thing

With and without

Without

泳ぐが好き。 ✗

Broken. Like "I like swim". が needs a thing, and 泳ぐ is an action.

With の

泳ぐのが好き。

"I like swimming." の turns the action into a thing.

Without

母がケーキを作った。

"Mom made the cake." Just the plain fact.

With の

ケーキを作ったのは母だ。

"The one who made the cake? Mom." Same fact, but the answer is saved for the end.

When to add な

Noun or な-adjective right before の? Put な in between. Verbs and い-adjectives get nothing.

Verb / い-adjective

泳ぐのが好き。　寒いのが苦手。

Nothing added.

Noun / な-adjective

静かなのがいい。

"Quiet is good." Add な.
