What it is

The official Japanese test, run by the Japan Foundation and JEES, in Japan and many other countries. There are five levels: N5 is the easiest, N1 the hardest.

  • Only reading and listening. It's a multiple choice answer sheet with no speaking or writing. Most questions have four choices, some listening ones have three.
  • You pick your level. The official site doesn't ask for a lower level first, it just says to choose one that suits you. There's no age limit.
  • The certificate never expires, though a company or school can set its own limit.

N5 and N4 mainly test basic Japanese learned in class. N3 is the bridge up to N2 and N1.

Times can change a little, listening especially.

On N2 and N1, vocabulary, grammar and reading are one part with one timer, and you choose how long to spend on each. On N5, N4 and N3, vocabulary is a separate part with its own timer.

You always take every part: you can't retake only the part you failed, and missing a part means you fail.

Passing

You need two things: enough points in total, and the minimum in every score. The scores are split differently on the lower levels.

Total, out of 180Minimum in each score
N580Vocabulary, grammar and reading: 38 of 120
Listening: 19 of 60
N490Vocabulary, grammar and reading: 38 of 120
Listening: 19 of 60
N395Vocabulary and grammar, reading, listening: 19 of 60 each
N290Vocabulary and grammar, reading, listening: 19 of 60 each
N1100Vocabulary and grammar, reading, listening: 19 of 60 each

So 100 points in total with 15 for listening is a fail, on every level. Scores are scaled, not a count of right answers, so there's no fixed number of correct answers that passes.

What to study

The JLPT hasn't published official lists since 2010, so every number here is an estimate. The ones you'll see most:

WordsKanji
N5about 800about 100
N4about 1,500about 300
N3about 3,750about 650
N2about 6,000about 1,000
N1about 10,000about 2,000

Each number includes the levels below it, and N1's word count varies the most between sources.

A simple order:

  1. See the format first. Try the official sample questions: N5, N4, N3, N2, N1.
  2. Learn the material. On this site: N5 grammar, the N5 vocabulary list and the N5 kanji.
  3. Listen every week. It has its own minimum score, so strong reading can't make up for weak listening.
  4. Do timed practice. Near the test, use the Official Practice Workbook with a clock running.
Dates and deadlines

The test is twice a year, on a Sunday in July and December. Some cities outside Japan only hold one of them.

Sign-up months are a rough guide, because every test city sets its own deadline and fee.

Signing up: outside Japan, find your city and its local organizer in the official list of test cities, then follow their instructions and pay them. In Japan, it goes through the Japan sign-up page.

Results: everyone gets a score report, and if you pass, a certificate too.
