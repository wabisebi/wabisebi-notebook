Careful isn't the same as polite

ではなかった sounds stiff and formal. So why do textbooks call it the casual form? Because "polite" and "careful" are two different things in Japanese.

Take one sentence:

休みじゃなかった。

"It wasn't a day off." There are four ways to say this. All four mean exactly the same thing. Only the tone changes.

Two separate choices

1. Polite or not? Is there a です at the end? This is about who you're talking to. Add です and you're being polite to the listener. Leave it out and you're not.

2. Relaxed or careful? Do you say じゃ or では? This is about how carefully you word it. じゃ is just では said quickly. Same meaning.

The two choices don't affect each other, so you get four combinations.

English does the same

Think of "sir" as the politeness:

RelaxedCareful
No "sir"It wasn't.It was not.
With "sir"It wasn't, sir.It was not, sir.

"Sir" is like です: respect for the person you're talking to. "Wasn't" vs "was not" is like じゃ vs では: just how carefully you say it. "It was not." sounds careful, but it isn't polite. Nobody says it to be respectful to their boss.

The same four in Japanese

Relaxed (じゃ)Careful (では)
No ですじゃなかったではなかった
With ですじゃなかったですではなかったです

じゃなかった: talking to a friend.
ではなかった: books, articles, or a big dramatic line in a film. Nobody to be polite to, but carefully worded.
じゃなかったです: polite, but friendly and relaxed.
ではなかったです: polite and careful.

One extra: for polite and careful, people usually say ではありませんでした instead of ではなかったです. Same meaning, same tone. It's the version you hear on the news.

So why "casual"?

In textbooks, "casual" (or "plain") only means no です. It doesn't mean relaxed. ではなかった has no です, so it counts as casual, even though it sounds careful. (On this site too: see じゃなかった.)

To remember: です at the end decides polite. じゃ or では decides relaxed or careful.
